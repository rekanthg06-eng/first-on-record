## IMPORTANT CAVEAT FOR THE SCRIPT
8.087 g matches the LIGHT stater (about 8.2 g) not the original HEAVY stater (about 10.7-10.9 g). One source (colorado.edu Expressions of Identity page) says the heavy staters came first and the lighter issues "were likely a Persian continuation minted at Sardis". The Met nevertheless dates this coin 560-546. So do not say "Croesus made this coin" and avoid claiming it is from the very first issue. Safe: "a gold stater from Sardis, dated by the Met to about 560-546 BCE". The 10.7 g figure belongs to the heavy standard, not this coin. Do not quote 10.7 g as the weight of this coin.

## Story notes

### 1. Electrum and its problem
- Electrum is a natural gold-silver alloy; Lydian electrum coins appeared at the end of the 7th century BCE. [Met essay "Sardis", https://www.metmuseum.org/en/essays/sardis]
- The gold share in natural electrum is not fixed (65-85% gold in the natural state per Vox/CEPR article); neither weighing nor the touchstone could tell the exact share, so it looked a poor coinage metal. [CEPR, https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/reasons-lydian-electrum-coins-and-succeeding-greek-silver-coins-antiquity]
- Coins were standardised by weight, but because the gold-to-silver ratio varied, their value was unpredictable; Croesus split coinage into separate gold and silver denominations. [Live Science, https://www.livescience.com/archaeology/croesus-stater-the-2-500-year-old-coin-that-introduced-the-gold-standard] (note: Live Science says "father Alyattes made electrum coins"; I did not verify that independently)
- Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta: the mix in earlier electrum staters made their value inconsistent; Croesus stabilised the stater with "pure gold"; government vouched for weight and quality. [https://www.frbatlanta.org/about/tours/story-of-money/06-early-coins/gold-stater] (Fed museum page, secondary)
- Twist from Ramage and Craddock via BMCR review: natural Pactolus gold has about 17-30% silver, varying; Lydian electrum coins tested consistently at about 55% gold / 45% silver, most likely by adding silver to natural gold. So the issuer controlled the mix, the problem was that users could not check it. [BMCR 2001.08.10, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=22490] CEPR agrees on about 55% gold plus or minus 2%.
- Sardis Expedition essay: early electrum coins have higher silver than natural gold, suggesting silver was added; natural gold from the Sardis streams contains virtually no silver (the author's own hypothesis: the refinery handled old electrum coins/objects). [https://sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/latw-greenewalt-gold-silver-refining]

### 2. The Sardis gold refinery
- Location: Pactolus North (PN) sector, outside the city walls and near the Pactolus stream. [Sardis Expedition Greenewalt essay; Sardis Expedition PN page, https://sardisexpedition.org/en/essays/about-pn]
- Dating: first half of the 6th century BCE (Greenewalt, Sardis Expedition). BMCR: Ramage dates the key period ca. 575-550 BCE; pottery mid-6th c.
- Process: electrum sheets and grains packed in salt (with brick dust) inside coarse pottery containers, heated in small brick ovens at roughly 800 C (BMCR: 600-800 C) for many hours, possibly days; salt vapour turned silver to silver chloride, absorbed by clay and brick; gold left "basically pure". Silver then recovered by smelting with lead and cupellation in bowl hearths (up to about 1100 C). [Greenewalt; Sardis PN page; BMCR]
- Finds: as many as 200 bowl hearths exposed (Greenewalt); "hundreds of cupels" (PN page); a cementation furnace with a cooking pot excavated; a lead litharge cake. [PN page]
- Earliest known evidence for cementation anywhere (Greenewalt, Sardis Expedition). Ramage in Harvard Magazine: salt the major parting agent; process not found elsewhere. [https://www.harvardmagazine.com/sites/default/files/html/1998/03/sardis3.html]
- Discovery anecdote (Harvard Magazine): Ramage noticed green ring-shaped depressions in a clay floor on July 6; Dick Stone identified cupels; Halis Aydintasbas spotted the first tiny gold speck. (Year not given in the fetched summary; Harvard Magazine 1998 issue.)
- Small altar of schist built shortly after, probably for Kuvava (Cybele), with small limestone lions on its corners (PN page). Nice visual, one source only.
- BMCR: refinery was not associated with a mint; it did bulk purification. So the dossier cannot say the coins were struck at the refinery.

### 3. Pure gold and pure silver, same design
- Met essay: Croesus (ruled roughly 560-546 BCE) is credited with issuing the first coins of pure gold and pure silver. Met says "credited", keep that hedge.
- Greenewalt: pure gold and pure silver coins appeared "shortly before the middle of the sixth century BC (during the rule of Croesus)".
- Both gold and silver coins show the foreparts of a lion and a bull; the same dies could be used for both metals (colorado.edu page; Live Science).
- Weights (colorado.edu page): heavy standard gold and silver staters about 10.9 g, plus fractions; later light gold staters about 8.2 g and silver sigloi about 5.4 g. Live Science: first coins standardised at 10.7 g. LBMA: one Croesus stater 10.72 g, 18 x 16 x 4 mm (different coin in the LBMA collection).
- Hesperia 2025: nine silver croeseids found at Sardis with the bones of their owner in the 547 BCE destruction level, heavily worn (more than 14 years circulation, authors say), supporting a Croesus reign start in the 580s (Wallace) rather than 561. [https://ascsa.edu.gr/publications/hesperia/article/94/2/143-183] This is vivid but the abstract only.

### 4. Lion and bull; how it was struck
- Lion and bull foreparts confronted (Met description).
- Meaning: may reflect a common Near Eastern motif, or each animal may stand for one of the two metals (Live Science, speculation, not fact).
- Reverse: square punch marks, two on gold staters and one on silver (Live Science). Single source; the Met page text I got did not describe the reverse.
- Size of 26.59.2: 1 x 1.6 cm per Met. Live Science compares first coins to a dime (a dime is about 17.9 mm across, my own knowledge, not from sources). A "fingernail" comparison is MY inference from 1.6 cm; not sourced. Say "about 1.6 cm long, smaller than a thumbnail" at most, or use "about the size of a small bean/dime"; flag for editor.

### 5. Croesus attribution debate
- Tradition back to Herodotus links lion-and-bull coins to Croesus. Michael Vickers (1985) argued they were issued under Darius; Margaret Cool Root (1988) rejected this using the Persepolis evidence; the colorado.edu article concludes lion-and-bull coins first appeared in Croesus's reign and that the lighter later issues were likely a Persian continuation at Sardis. [https://www.colorado.edu/project/expressionsofidentity/media/455] (single source for the debate details; Price 1984 "Croesus hoard: hoard or hoax" PDF showed up in search but I did not read it)
- BMCR: bimetallic system credited to Croesus "commonly (although not unanimously)" accepted.
- Hesperia 2025 (Cahill, Kroll et al.): attributes the silver croeseids to Croesus, based on the 547 BCE destruction level at Sardis. Does not discuss Persian attribution in the abstract.
- Met: Croesus "credited". LBMA: Lydia fell to Cyrus 546 BCE; the system survived in the Persian empire.
- Note: destruction-level date varies between sources (546 per Met/LBMA/colorado, 547 per Hesperia).

### 6. Herodotus
- Histories 1.94, tr. G. C. Macaulay: "and they were the first of men, so far as we know, who struck and used coin of gold or silver." [https://lexundria.com/hdt/1.94/mcly]. Met essay paraphrases it: Herodotus says the Lydians were the first people to mint coins, date in dispute.
- Caveat: Herodotus' line is about Lydians generally, hedged with "so far as we know". Wording is Macaulay's translation.
- The Perseus URL fetch was blocked (permission not granted); I relied on lexundria. A second text (cts.perseids.org) appeared in search results but not read.

### 7. Vivid human angles (sourced)
- Wealth from the Pactolus River gold; "rich as Croesus" (Met essay; Live Science).
- Silver coins owned by someone who died in the burning of Sardis in 546/547 BCE (Hesperia).
- Refinery had a little altar with lions, perhaps a thank-offering for refining success (PN page).
